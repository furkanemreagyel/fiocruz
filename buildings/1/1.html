Moorish Pavilion

The Moorish Pavilion (Fiocruz Castle, 1905-1918), or Castle, the main building of the set, is the headquarters and greatest symbol of the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation. The building was built on the highest level of the largest hill on the site, with its main facade facing the ocean to influence natural ventilation by capturing the sea breeze. It was designed in Moorish style of architecture—rich in ornamentation, with the Alhambra in Granada (Spain) being its main inspiration. At the time of its inauguration, the Moorish Pavilion was one of the buildings with the greatest technological sophistication in the country. Currently, in addition to the Presidency and units linked to it, the Moorish Pavilion houses the Rare Works collection of the Biomedical Sciences Library.

Reference: Fundação Oswaldo Cruz. (n.d.). Pavilhão Mourisco: O castelo da Fiocruz [Photograph]. Casa de Oswaldo Cruz. https://portal.fiocruz.br/pavilhão-mourisco

Oswaldo Cruz made the first sketches of the building, which already indicated the style of ornamentation. The Portuguese architect Luiz Moraes Júnior was invited by the scientist for the project, which should house laboratories, offices and a library.  

Construction began in 1904 and ended in 1918. Deceased early, in 1917, at the age of 44, Oswaldo did not see the completion of the work, but worked in the building's laboratories, which were already occupied by scientists in 1910.

The Moorish Pavilion fuses early 20th-century aesthetic eclecticism with cutting-edge industrial infrastructure. Designed for microbiology and serum production, the building integrated advanced utility systems, including electricity, piped gas, and modern sewage,  alongside specialized scientific zones for micro-photography and a dedicated power plant. Its construction utilized high-performance imported materials like copper domes, steel, and intricate tile work (Portuguese azulejos), which served the purpose of creating sterile laboratory environments and providing natural climate control for Rio de Janeiro’s tropical heat. Validated by sources like UNESCO and SciELO, the Pavilion stands as a landmark where Neo-Moorish architectural beauty was intentionally engineered to facilitate the birth of modern tropical medicine in Brazil.
